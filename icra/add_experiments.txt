\textbf{Instance level:} a real/substituted pair is \emph{ranked correctly}
when the two trajectories' collision outcomes actually differ and $R$'s
ordering agrees with which one collided; ties (no outcome change) carry no
ranking signal and are reported separately. Table~\ref{tab:planner_screening}:
UniAD and VAD rank the large majority of their non-tied pairs correctly
($9/11$, $8/10$); TCP agrees on $2$ of its $3$ non-tied pairs, too few to
draw a rate from; AD-MLP inverts this almost entirely ($3/15$ correct,
$12/15$ backwards), the $15$-scenario aggregate confirmation that the
single failure diagnosed in Sec.~\ref{sec:exp_case_studies} is systematic
to AD-MLP on this scenario family. Fig.~\ref{fig:case_study_planner_ranking}
shows the mirror image: the best-separated UniAD pair, where $R$ correctly
separates a flat, comfortable-margin real trajectory ($R=0.037$) from a
substituted one whose margin collapses and hazard climbs to $0.52$ by
$t=4$s ($R=0.914$).

\subsection{Case Study: A Diagnosable Failure}
\label{sec:exp_case_studies}

Beyond a qualitative pair of collision-free instances (large sustained
margin vs.\ brief close call, below), we construct
one failure case designed to stress-test $R$ against ground truth stronger
than a recorded label: we run the released AD-MLP
checkpoint~\cite{bench2drivezoo} open-loop (no CARLA) on a Bench2Drive
\emph{AccidentTwoWays} scenario and substitute its predicted trajectory for
ego, keeping every other agent's real recorded track unchanged. AD-MLP
badly under-predicts the expert's actual acceleration here (mean $12$m
error over 3s); the resulting counterfactual ego stalls into another
tracked agent's path and genuinely collides ($\tau_{\mathrm{3D}}\!=\!0$,
$m\!=\!-3.05$ at $t\!=\!1.7$s) -- yet CARM scores it $R=0.106$, \emph{lower}
than the real, truly collision-free expert trajectory's $R=0.956$
(Fig.~\ref{fig:case_study_failure}). This is diagnosable: panel (c) shows
$q_k$ genuinely rising for the counterfactual ($0.66$ by $t=4$s vs.\
$\approx\!0$ for the real trajectory) -- the hazard head detects the danger
-- but the composition (Eq.~\ref{eq:carm_composition}) still yields a low
$R$: the event mass accumulates almost entirely in the last bin ($F_k$
stays near $0$ until $t\!\approx\!4$s), so a hazard head that is otherwise
detecting real danger still contributes little to the aggregated risk once
weighted by how late it fires. This suggests the learned safe-TTC $T_s$
may be biased toward late critical-event times on this kind of
low-closing-speed, delayed-collision interaction -- a hypothesis this one
diagnosed instance supports directly, but that would need the
learned-correction visualization described below to confirm at scale; we
do not otherwise have aggregate evidence for it yet.

\subsection{Method-Level Ranking Consistency (Not Yet Run)}
\label{sec:method_ranking_not_run}

Instance-level agreement (Sec.~\ref{sec:exp_consistency}) does not by itself
establish that FutureRisk can replace collision rate for its most common
practical use: ranking \emph{methods}, not individual trajectories. The
protocol: for a set of driving policies evaluated on the same Bench2Drive
scenarios, compare (a) each policy's empirical collision rate against (b)
the mean and $95^{\mathrm{th}}$ percentile of $R$ over that policy's
rollouts, reporting Spearman's $\rho$ and Kendall's $\tau$ between the two
rankings. This needs closed-loop rollouts from multiple independent
policies on shared Bench2Drive scenarios, which requires a CARLA-based
rollout pipeline for third-party planners not yet built, so it has not
been run.

\subsection{Learned Safe-TTC and Component/Aggregation/Label Ablations (Not Yet Run)}

Four further analyses from the original design remain unrun, protocol
only: (1) visualizing the learned correction
$\exp(\Delta_{\max}\tanh(g_\mu(\bm h)))$ -- the multiplicative factor CARM
applies to $\tau_{\mathrm{phy}}$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:adaptive_safe_ttc}) -- binned
by interaction type and relative closing speed, checking whether its
direction matches intuition (e.g., a larger margin for oncoming than
car-following at the same closing speed); (2) a component ablation adding
pipeline stages one at a time -- raw 3D-TTC, the analytic margin, the
hazard head with $\tau_{\mathrm{safe}}$ fixed to $\tau_{\mathrm{phy}}$,
learned safe-TTC (Stage 1), and the bounded residual (full CARM) --
reporting consistency and fine-grained discrimination at each step; (3) an
agent-aggregation ablation replacing the noisy-OR rule
(Eq.~\ref{eq:candidate_risk_aggregation}) with the max and mean per-agent
risk, holding everything else fixed; and (4) a critical-event label
ablation comparing the hazard head trained on the full $T_{\mathrm{evt}}$
label (collision $\lor$ evasive-severity proxy, Eq.~\ref{eq:carm_critical_event})
against one trained on collision events alone, given that DeepAccident's
native ground truth already makes true collisions far less sparse than in
a planner-driven corpus like Bench2Drive (Sec.~\ref{sec:training-data}).
